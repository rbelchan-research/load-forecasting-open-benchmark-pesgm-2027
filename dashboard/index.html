01

What is in the benchmark

Four substations from the SMART-DS Greensboro models, two urban and two rural, with the full secondary network intact. That means every service transformer comes with the customers actually connected behind it and its nameplate rating, which is what makes a loading study possible at all. Load is hourly apparent power in kVA for a full calendar year of 2018 weather.

Why a published synthetic system rather than measured utility data. Studying transformer loading needs the secondary network, the customer to transformer mapping and the rating of every unit, and public metering releases carry meter series without any of that. A controlled sweep across grouping methods, group counts and coverage tiers needs thousands of units from one coherent system, and a benchmark other people are meant to extend has to sit on data they can download. Inside the dataset the weather year is measured, the profiles come from building simulation rather than curve fitting, and the transformer loading comes from an OpenDSS power flow on the electrical model, so household behavior is the only modeled link in the chain. Section 6 of the FAQ sets out the full argument and what it costs. The models and profiles are published through the Department of Energy's Open Energy Data Initiative as submission 2981 under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 license, so the whole test system is free to download, redistribute and build on with credit.

02

Turning metered kilowatts into transformer loading

A loading screen compares apparent power against a rating in kVA, but a meter reports real power in kW. We ran a full year of fifteen minute power flow in OpenDSS on 1,154 transformers to find out what the usual flat power factor costs, and the answer turned out to matter more than any modeling choice in the rest of the study.

The headline. A flat ratio flags 23 transformers as overloaded on the rural substation where the power flow finds 3. Summed customer kilowatts finds none of them. A three parameter calibration gets 3 of 3 and 5 of 5 exactly right.

Measured kVA per kW, by loading level

The ratio is not a constant. It runs near 1.25 when the transformer is lightly loaded and falls toward 1.09 at the hours that decide the screen.

Does the choice of grouping method matter

Seventeen settings with everything else held fixed. Five methods cluster the metered history, and two need no meter data at all. The whole spread is 0.4 percentage points, which is less than the difference between two random samples of the fleet.

One dot per setting, with the group count swept from 2 to 32 where the method allows it. The two methods that need no meter data land among the ones that read a full year of it.

06

Accurate forecasts do not catch overloads

This is the result with the most practical weight. Every model trained to minimize average error undershoots the daily peak, so when the forecast is used to decide which transformer to inspect it almost never raises a flag, no matter how much electric vehicle load is added.

Screening quality as electric vehicles arrive

The share of transformers past nameplate climbs from 4.3 to 30.6 percent across this range. The accurate models stay flat at zero.

What damaged meter history costs

Four kinds of fault injected into the history the models learn from, repaired the way a utility would by filling from the same hour one week earlier, then scored against the clean series. Gaps turn out to be close to harmless. Undetected spikes are not.

08

Rooftop solar and home batteries

Both were added as overlays on top of the measured fleet, with every parameter stated so the scenario can be replaced. They pull in opposite directions, and the battery result is the one worth arguing about at a conference.

Rooftop solar

Solar makes the hourly series harder to forecast while making the loading problem easier, since generation shaves the afternoon.

Home batteries on a fixed daily schedule

Batteries make the series easier to forecast and the loading problem worse, because every battery on the street charges in the same overnight window.

09

Seasons, hours and weather

This fleet peaks in both seasons, which is part of why it makes a good test system. Seventy one percent of transformers reach their annual peak between May and September and twenty seven percent reach it between November and March.

Average weekday shape by season

Each series is the fleet mean load for that season, divided by the annual mean.

Fleet load against outdoor temperature

Load bottoms out in the mild band and rises in both directions, by about a quarter on the cold side and by double on the hot side.

Two clear modes, one in January and one across June and July.

Where the method works and where it does not

A fleet median is of little use to someone deciding whether to trust the result on one particular feeder, so the fleet is split along properties a planner can check before running anything. Error falls as the load flattens and as more customers sit behind the unit.
